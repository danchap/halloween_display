night after Daniel's answers (everything shipped to the island, no hooks

## The build in one paragraph

Eight legs of PVC evacuation pipe Ø 40 mm in black foam sleeves, bent at
the knee by a fixed PVC elbow or a pair of them, meet a spine of Ø 40
pipe with four cross fittings inside the body. Each foot stands on a
vertical post of Ø 50 pipe, and the four posts of a side plug into a
Ø 50 pipe lying along the base of that wall (the ski), so nothing stands
in the alley. Each ski runs 1.5 m past its end posts; two cords from
each post top go through one hole at the top, one down to a hole near
the near end of the ski and one down round the ski at the far post's
tee, each with a tensioner, and twelve sandbags sit on the skis where
the cords pull up. A 2 m piece of Ø 40 pipe inside each of the four
front posts stiffens them against a child hanging on a stay. The body
is black stretch fabric over a taped bin bag, shaped by pool-noodle
hoops zip-tied to the spine, with googly or lit eyes on the head. Every
joint that sets an angle is set by turning the fitting in its socket
and locked with two screws, so it comes apart with a screwdriver; the
knee elbows and the hub (spine, crosses, stubs and the hip elbow pairs)
are glued because they carry the most load, and each upper leg is
screwed into its hip pair. The walls step 2 to 7 cm at the feet along
the alley, so the page's "Posts plumb" setting (on by default) puts
each side's feet on one straight line at the innermost wall over the
whole run of the ski and every post stands vertical; the gap between
the ski's face and a wall that steps out is packed with offcuts, and at
the alley mouth, where the right wall turns away, the right ski's front
end lies 0.87 m off the wall in the open (the page's HUD says so).

## The bracing

Why the cords are there: a PVC post is a pipe standing in a tee. Pushed
along the wall at its top, an unbraced post folds at a screwed tee
under 10 to 15 N, and with all eight posts tilting together the spider
sways 0.45 m in a 50 km/h wind even with glued tees. Across the alley
the two sides push against the walls through the skis and the legs act
as an arch, so the cords only have to hold the posts along the walls.

Two independent structural passes on 13 October (E = 3 GPa for PVC,
Ø 50 × 3 mm posts, 4 mm polyester cord at about 10 kN per unit strain,
the page's default pose) settled how:

- Cords from each post top down to the base of the next post, both ways
  in every bay (the "X-braces" first asked for) do not work: the front
  post's top is held only against moving forward and the back post's
  only against moving backward, so each can swing the other way held by
  nothing but its tee (0.4 to 0.9 m of sway under 34 N), and the cords
  are so steep (59 to 66°) that every newton held costs two in the
  cord.
- Cords to loose sandbags on the ground past the skis (the earlier
  default) fail at the anchor: the pretension alone puts about 190 N of
  uplift and 150 N of pull on the front bag, so a 25 kg bag slides
  before any wind.
- What works: every post guyed both ways in the plane of its wall, one
  cord to the near end of the ski and one to the ski at the far post's
  tee, with the skis extended 1.5 m past the end posts so both anchors
  are the ski itself (cords at 28 to 63° from horizontal). Sway at a
  50 km/h wind along the alley: 18 / 12 / 10 / 10 mm at the four post
  tops; cord tensions 140 to 170 N; the posts' compression about
  200 N against an Euler load of 418 N for the 2.55 m front post
  (560 to 960 N for the others).
- The governing abuse is not the wind but a child hanging on a front
  stay: 30 kg mid-cord puts about 550 N of compression into the front
  post, more than its Euler load. Hence the two rules the page now
  enforces with a warning: no foot higher than 2.6 m with the stays,
  and a 2 m length of Ø 40 pipe centred inside each post of the two
  front pairs (two tubes one inside the other add their stiffness:
  610 N for a 2.55 m post, safety factor 1.5 on the child). An adult
  hanging on a cord or a foot still buckles a post: the adults are told
  on the night.
- Two cords per post through one 8 mm hole 40 mm below the post's top
  end, drilled along the wall, its edge rounded with a countersink;
  each cord ends in a figure-eight stopper knot on the far side of the
  hole. One continuous cord through the hole would equalise the two
  tensions, not the two horizontal pulls, and let the front post lean
  5 to 10 cm. The hole leaves 78 % of the pipe's stiffness, which is
  fine; the loop-under-the-coupler idea was rejected (it slides down
  the pipe).
- Pretension: set the shallow cord of each post to about 80 N (a firm
  pull by hand), then tighten the steep one until the post is plumb.
  Nothing is tensioned before the legs are on: a stayed post without
  its leg stands across the alley on its screwed socket alone. Every
  tensioner is backed with two half hitches, and all sixteen are
  checked each of the first three mornings (braided cord stretches 1
  to 3 % as it settles) and after any wind.
- Ballast: the cords pull the ski up where they anchor, so the sandbags
  go there: two 25 kg bags at each ski end and one just outside each
  end post's tee, twelve in all (300 kg of sand). Safety factor against
  lifting 1.9 to 2.2 at the ends and 1.2 to 1.4 at the end tees in a
  70 km/h gust; sliding of a ski along the alley holds a 50 km/h wind
  with a factor of 2.5 even on wet, slimy stone (friction 0.2). In a
  3 m alley between houses the wind along it is 1.2 to 1.4 times the
  forecast, so the take-down rule is read on the forecast gusts.
- Cord: 4 mm braided polyester with a reflective tracer (tent guyline),
  not black: the stays cross head height between the posts in a lane
  people walk at night. Polyester holds its length wet; a cord with a
  polypropylene core creeps and is not used. Every crossing of a stay
  and a post is zip-tied to the post.
- The 70 km/h rule stands: above a forecast of 70 km/h gusts the body
  comes down, or the whole spider.

The page draws all of it for the pose on screen (Side view shows the
stays best) and warns when a foot is above 2.6 m, when a stay is
steeper than 70°, and when a ski's end lies off its wall.

## What the research settled

- No adjustable or swivelling elbow exists in Ø 40 PVC (Nicoll,
  Interplast, Wirquin, Fitt, First Plast, Girpi, RIBON were checked).
  What exists is fixed elbows of 20°, 30°, 45°, 67.5° and 87.5°, a
  cuttable RIBON elbow (5/10/15° or 15/25/35°) and the Wirquin Magicoude
  bellows, which bends to any angle but has no stiffness. Any angle from
  0° to 155° comes from two fixed elbows in series, twisted against each
  other; the page computes which two and the twist for every joint, and
  the pairs for the default pose are tabled under Cut list below.
- Posts of Ø 40 pipe are too weak where children can reach: a child
  hanging on a 1.6 m foot or a 200 N push at mid height buckles or bends
  a 2.9 m post. Ø 50 × 3 mm posts stayed at the top have a safety factor
  of 2.5 in a 60 km/h wind and 1.5 on a 200 N push along the wall. The
  Amazon ZENLYS PN10 bars (2.4 mm wall) are too thin for a post; the
  PN16 bars (3.7 mm) and NF evacuation pipe (3 mm) are fine.
- The knee and hub joints carry 25 to 40 N m from the spider's own
  weight; a screwed push-fit socket holds 20 to 30 N m. Those joints are
  glued. The one screwed socket left at each hip (pair to upper leg)
  sees 15 to 25 N m, a safety factor of about 1, and every screwed
  socket has about 0.9° of play, so expect the hub to settle 10 to 25 cm
  below the page's height. Before the knees are glued, hang 5 kg at 1 m
  from one screwed and one glued elbow overnight and look for creep.
- Amazon.fr delivers to 17880 Les Portes-en-Ré: its own location check
  accepts the postcode, and every product page read with it set shows a
  dated delivery, one day later than Paris at most. Bulky items over
  175 cm sold by a marketplace seller ship by that seller's carrier to
  the kerb, with an adult present. The nearest Amazon locker is at the
  Leclerc in Saint-Martin-de-Ré, but lockers refuse marketplace items
  and anything over 55 cm.
- A plumbing web merchant, plomberie.fr (Bordeaux area, 05 47 14 00 77),
  sells 4 m NF evacuation bars singly, in stock, dispatched in 24 to
  48 h, bulky parcels delivered in 2 to 3 days anywhere in metropolitan
  France for 15.90 € (part B). Castorama delivers 2 m pipe to the door
  with the fee set by the largest single product (4.90 € under 2 m and
  30 kg), the date shown only at checkout. Brico Dépôt sends anything
  4 m long by crane truck within 15 days: not usable. Leroy Merlin's
  site could not be read from here (it blocks automated readers), so
  its home-delivery terms for 4 m pipe are unverified.
- If anything is built in Paris after all: Chronopost takes a box up to
  150 cm long (length plus twice width plus twice height under 300 cm,
  30 kg) with free collection at the flat, next working day, about 29 €
  a box; Mail Boxes Etc. packs for you and ships by UPS up to 274 cm.
  The page's "Longest piece delivered" has 1.4 m and 2.7 m for those.

How a pair makes an angle: two elbows a and b plugged together give any
bend between a − b and a + b. Twisting the second elbow about the
first's outlet by ψ gives the bend θ with cos θ = cos a · cos b −
sin a · sin b · cos ψ; the page prints ψ. The pair is then turned as a
whole in the socket that holds it, to point the leg: at the hip this
sets how far up the leg rises, at the foot the post's own rotation in
the ski tee sets the leg's direction along the alley. Mark the position
with a pen, drill 3 mm through socket and spigot, and drive two 4.2 × 16
screws per socket at 90° apart.

## Assembly, in order

0. Optional, in Paris, with nothing to ship: buy two 45° elbows, a
   coupler, a metre of Ø 40 pipe and a small tin of cement at any
   Castorama, make one glued and one screwed joint, hang 5 kg at 1 m
   from each overnight, and learn how the cement behaves. Nothing else
   is built in Paris.
1. Island, day 1 (the day after the last parcel): check every part
   against the lists. Build the hub: cut the spine pieces and the stubs
   from one Ø 40 bar, push the four crosses on with the spine pieces
   between them, glue them. Set each hip pair to its twist from the
   page's cut list (stub into the 45's socket, the 45's spigot into the
   20's socket), glue the two elbows together, glue the pair onto its
   stub and glue a Ø 40 coupler onto the pair's spigot; the coupler's
   free socket takes the upper leg. An adult glues, outdoors, away from
   the children; the cement takes a day before it carries load. Cut
   the leg pieces to the list (the kids measure and mark, an adult
   cuts); leave the posts uncut. Slide a foam sleeve over each leg
   piece before any fitting goes on. Fit each knee: the elbow or pair at
   its twist, the coupler on its spigot, glued to both pipes; the legs
   rest until day 3. Fit the foot elbow pair, screwed. Sort the
   fittings per leg into eight labelled bags.
2. Day 2: skis, nothing standing. Drill the 8 mm hole 50 mm from each
   ski end and the 8 mm hole 40 mm below the top of each post bar,
   along the wall; round every hole's edge. Lay each ski along its
   wall: cap, 1.5 m extension, tee, pipe, tee, pipe, tee, pipe, tee,
   1.5 m extension, cap. Screw every socket. Fill the sacks (25 kg
   each) and set them on the skis: two at each end, one just outside
   each end tee, all against the wall. Dry-fit one post stack (Ø 50
   coupler, reducer, a foot pair), measure from the pipe stop in the
   coupler to the foot pair's centre, and cut each post to put its foot
   at its height; slide the 2 m liner into each front post (pairs 1 and
   2) before its coupler goes on; the posts lie along the skis until
   their legs go on.
3. Day 3: the hub goes up, three adults, the children out of the alley
   until the eighth leg is screwed and every post stayed. Prop the hub
   on the spare Ø 50 length cut to 2.3 m with a cap below and a tee on
   top cradling the spine, standing on a sack under the body, steadied
   by one adult; the ladders are for the two fitting the legs. One leg
   at a time, the long back pairs first, alternating sides: push the
   upper leg into its hip pair and screw it; push the reducer (with its
   coupler) onto the foot pair's spigot; lift the post into the coupler,
   lower it into its tee, turn it to the leg's plan angle, drill and
   screw tee, coupler and reducer; pass that post's two cords through
   its hole (a stopper knot on the far side of each), one to the hole
   at the near ski end, one round the ski at the far tee, each with its
   tensioner at the bottom, hand-tight only. When both posts of a pair
   stand, zip-tie two Ø 40 offcuts across them as rungs, one near the
   top and one at 1 m. The prop comes out after the eighth leg. Then
   the tension: on each post the shallow cord to a firm hand pull
   (about 80 N), the steep one until the post is plumb; two half
   hitches behind every tensioner; a zip tie where a stay crosses a
   post.
4. Day 4: the body. Bin bag over the spine, taped shut; hoops zip-tied
   to the spine; fabric over everything, slits taped at the eight legs;
   the eyes on their discs or balls; fangs from two white pipe offcuts,
   blunt. Reflective tape on the post bases.
5. Dry run two nights before: walk under it, push a post along the wall
   and then toward the alley centre, check every screw and every
   tensioner, leave it up overnight and check again in the morning.
   Check the tensioners again on each of the first three mornings.

## Safety rules

- Every post is stayed by two cords in the plane of its wall from the
  moment its leg is on: to the near ski end and to the far tee, with
  the sandbags on the skis where the cords pull up; the two posts of a
  pair are joined by two rungs. The page draws the stays and counts the
  cord and the bags.
- Keep every foot between 2.0 and 2.6 m: the page warns outside that
  range. A foot at 1.6 m is a handle for a child; a foot above 2.6 m
  stands on a post that a child hanging on its stay would buckle. The
  four front posts carry a liner for the same reason. An adult hanging
  on a foot or a cord still buckles a Ø 50 post: nobody hangs on
  either, and the adults are told so on the night.
- No pretension before the legs are on; never leave a stayed post
  standing without its leg.
- Knee elbows and the hub glued; two screws in every other socket;
  check all screws and all tensioners every morning, PVC joints work
  loose in wind and braided cord settles.
- Cap every open pipe end. No screw tips outside the pipe. No cord
  below 2.3 m across the alley. The stays run along the walls in the
  wall plane, cross head height between the posts, and rise from the
  ski ends at 28 to 63°: reflective cord, reflective tape on the post
  bases, a light on the display, and the bags at the ski ends as the
  visible start of each line.
- A push toward the alley centre is held by the leg acting as a strut
  (about 100 N) and the arch of the legs against the far wall; the
  cords give nothing across the alley. Capped ends and the foam mean
  nothing shatters. The dry run tests this push.
- Above a forecast of 70 km/h gusts (rafales; the alley speeds the
  wind along it by 1.2 to 1.4), take the body down or the whole spider
  down. Name who watches the forecast (Météo-France vigilance for
  Charente-Maritime) and who takes it down if Daniel is away.
- Wet stone with algae halves the skis' grip: the twelve bags are
  sized for it. Beach sand is prohibited; buy it.
- The mairie may ask for a civil-liability attestation (responsabilité
  civile).
